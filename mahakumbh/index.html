My Journey to
Maha Kumbh 2025

February 4, 2025 · 6 min read · by Sitansu Acharya

On February 4, 2025 — Basant Panchami, the most auspicious Shahi Snan of Maha Kumbh — I stood at the Triveni Sangam in Prayagraj, where the Ganga, Yamuna, and the invisible Saraswati converge. Around me: forty million people. Within me: a stillness I have rarely felt.

This is an attempt to put into words what is, at its core, beyond them.

Why I went

I am not a deeply religious man in the conventional sense. I don't observe every ritual or follow every fast. But I am spiritual — I believe that certain places and certain moments carry an energy that the rational mind cannot fully account for. Kumbh has always felt like one of those places.

Maha Kumbh occurs every twelve years. This one — 2025 — was predicted to draw the largest human gathering in recorded history. I had followed the news with growing wonder. Something in me said: you need to be there. Not as a tourist. As a participant.

So I booked the train, arranged accommodation in a dharamshala near the sangam, and went.

The arrival

Nothing prepares you for the scale. From the train, you can see it building — the tent cities stretching for kilometres, the ghats lit with diyas, the low hum that forty million human voices produce when they gather in a single place for a single purpose.

I arrived the evening before Basant Panchami. The streets were so dense with pilgrims that movement was measured in feet per minute. And yet there was no panic, no aggression. People were patient with each other in a way that you rarely see in a crowd. Perhaps because everyone there understood why they had come.

When everyone shares a purpose, the crowd becomes a congregation.

The morning of Shahi Snan

I woke at 3 AM. The air was cold — perhaps 8°C — and the ghats were already packed. The processions of Naga Sadhus had begun: ash-smeared, dreadlocked, carrying trishuls, moving through the crowd with an authority that parts people like water.

I joined the queue for the sangam. It took two hours to reach the water. When I finally stepped in — waist-deep in the cold, sacred confluence — I understood why people make this journey across lifetimes.

It was not a religious moment for me, exactly. It was something older and more fundamental than religion. It was the feeling of being infinitesimally small and, in that smallness, completely at peace. The river doesn't care who you are. It receives everyone equally. There is a democracy to the Ganges that no institution can fully replicate.

What I carried home

The days after Kumbh were quiet. I found myself less reactive to small frustrations, more patient in traffic, more present in conversations. Whether that was the effect of the experience or simply the natural result of a few days of sleep deprivation and cold water, I cannot say with certainty.

But I think it was more than that.

There are experiences that recalibrate you. That remind you of what scale actually looks like, of how much human life and longing and devotion exists beyond the small radius of your own concerns. Kumbh was that for me.

Standing in forty million people teaches you, strangely, how alone you usually are — and how unnecessary that is.

A note on faith

You don't have to be Hindu to be moved by Kumbh. You don't have to believe in any particular theology. What you need is an openness to the idea that humans, gathered in collective intention, produce something that is genuinely greater than the sum of their parts.

I saw people from every walk of life — farmers and software engineers, grandmothers and teenagers, sanyasis who had walked from the Himalayas and urban professionals on a long weekend. All of them, for a moment, in the same river. All of them looking for the same thing, even if they named it differently.

That, more than anything, is what I will remember.

Will I go again?

Maha Kumbh returns in twelve years. I will be fifty-something. I hope my knees hold. I hope I go.

Some places deserve to be returned to. The Triveni Sangam is one of them.
